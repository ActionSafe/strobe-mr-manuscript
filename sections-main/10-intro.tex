\subsection{Background}
Mendelian randomization (MR) is an analytical method that uses genetic variants, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, thereby reducing unmeasured confounding that often biases traditional observational studies \cite{smith_mendelian_2003, Nguyen2024, Davies2018}. Over the past two decades, extensions of MR methods \cite{Burgess2015, burgess_guidelines_2023, burgess_network_2015}, software packages \cite{hemani_mr-base_2018}, and public datasets \cite{sudlow_uk_2015} have emerged. The number of published MR studies has also increased substantially \cite{He2025}, reflecting the growing popularity of the approach in epidemiology and biomedical research.

However, concerns have been raised about the completeness, transparency, and reproducibility of MR reporting. Many studies provide insufficient methodological detail, which limits their interpretability and undermines the credibility of causal claims \cite{davies_issues_2013,islam_reporting_2022}. For example, IVs used in an MR study must satisfy three core assumptions: relevance, independence, and exclusion \cite{VanderWeele2014}. Authors should therefore report whether these assumptions were assessed and whether the results were robust to violations of the assumptions. As two-sample MR studies have become more common, additional concerns have also been raised about the comparability of genetic variant-exposure associations between the exposure and outcome samples. To address these issues, the STROBE-MR (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology for Mendelian Randomization) guideline was developed. The guideline contains 43 subitems (hereafter referred to as items for simplicity) across six sections: Title and Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, and Other Information. It provides a structured framework to help authors report MR studies clearly and to help reviewers assess reporting quality \cite{skrivankova_strengthening_2021}.

Despite the guideline's availability, assessments of MR study quality remain largely manual, relying on expert reviewers to evaluate adherence to STROBE-MR criteria \cite{li_causal_2025}. This manual approach limits large-scale assessment of MR reporting. Consequently, there is a need for automated methods that can efficiently and accurately evaluate reporting quality at scale.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities: these models can process and interpret complex biomedical texts efficiently, demonstrating strong potential in biomedical knowledge extraction \cite{legate_semiautomated_2024}, literature selection \cite{laignelot_large_2026}, risk of bias assessment \cite{nyrhi_large_2026, lai_assessing_2024}, and reporting quality assessment \cite{srinivasan_large_2025, luo_using_2025}. In this study, we developed an LLM-based approach to address the challenges of large-scale reporting quality assessment and evaluated its performance on an annotated dataset. We then applied this approach to more than 13,000 MR studies published between 2007 and 2025 to analyze reporting trends across different items, sections, and time periods.

We first describe the collection of MR studies, the strategy used to divide STROBE-MR items into smaller components, and the prompt design and model evaluation process. We then present the model performance and the state of MR reporting. Finally, we discuss implications for improving reporting quality and prompt design for assessing MR reporting or similar tasks.

\subsection{Research objectives}
This project aims to leverage LLMs to develop and validate an automated system capable of rapidly assessing the reporting quality of MR publications based on the STROBE-MR guideline. Such a system would enable us to describe the overall state of MR reporting and provide insights into better reporting practices and more reliable causal inference in epidemiology.
