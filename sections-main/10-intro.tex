% \subsection{Background}
Mendelian randomization (MR) is an analytical method that uses genetic variants, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, thereby reducing unmeasured confounding that often biases traditional observational studies \cite{smith_mendelian_2003, Nguyen2024, Davies2018}. Over the past two decades, extensions of MR methods \cite{Burgess2015, burgess_guidelines_2023, burgess_network_2015}, software packages \cite{hemani_mr-base_2018}, and public datasets \cite{sudlow_uk_2015} have emerged. The number of published MR studies has also increased substantially \cite{He2025}, reflecting the growing popularity of the approach in epidemiology and biomedical research. However, concerns have been raised about the completeness, transparency, and reproducibility of MR reporting \cite{skrivankova_strengthening_2021}. Many studies provide insufficient methodological detail, which limits their interpretability and undermines the credibility of causal claims \cite{davies_issues_2013,islam_reporting_2022}. For example, IVs used in an MR study must satisfy three core assumptions: relevance, independence, and exclusion \cite{VanderWeele2014}. Authors should therefore report whether these assumptions were assessed and whether the results were robust to violations of the assumptions. As two-sample MR studies have become more common, additional concerns have also been raised about the comparability of genetic variant-exposure associations between the exposure and outcome samples. To address these issues, the STROBE-MR (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology for Mendelian Randomization) guideline was developed. The guideline contains 43 sub-items (hereafter referred to as items for simplicity) across six sections: Title and Abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, and Other Information.

STROBE-MR provides a structured framework to help authors report MR studies clearly. It also offers reviewers a useful tool for assessing and identifying studies with high-quality reporting that can be included in evidence synthesis and meta-analysis \cite{zuo_systematic_2026, cui_genetically_2025}. Despite the guideline's availability, assessments of MR reporting quality remain largely manual, relying on expert reviewers to evaluate adherence to STROBE-MR criteria \cite{li_causal_2025}. For example, one study \cite{gibson_reporting_2023} assessed MR reporting manually and concluded that the quality of MR articles needs to be improved, but its work was limited to 116 articles using UK Biobank data. This manual approach limits large-scale assessment of MR reporting. Consequently, there is a need for automated methods that can efficiently and accurately evaluate reporting quality at scale.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities: these models can process and interpret complex biomedical texts efficiently, demonstrating strong potential in biomedical knowledge extraction \cite{legate_semiautomated_2024}, literature selection \cite{laignelot_large_2026}, risk of bias assessment \cite{nyrhi_large_2026, lai_assessing_2024}, and reporting quality assessment \cite{srinivasan_large_2025, luo_using_2025}.

In this study, we developed an LLM-based approach to address the challenges of large-scale reporting quality assessment and evaluated its performance on an annotated dataset. We collected full texts and supplementary materials for more than 13,000 MR studies published between 2007 and 2025 and applied this approach to analyze reporting trends across different STROBE-MR items, sections, and time periods. Unlike previous studies that classified complex STROBE-MR items as "partially reported" \cite{gibson_reporting_2023}, we decomposed compound items into more granular components, enabling a more detailed and informative assessment of reporting quality.

This project aims to leverage LLMs to develop and validate an automated system capable of rapidly assessing the reporting quality of MR publications based on the STROBE-MR guideline. Such a system would enable large-scale evaluation of reporting practices across the MR literature and help evidence synthesis researchers screen MR articles with transparent reporting. It also has the potential to support journals and editors in identifying reporting problems early in the publication process and to help authors improve the completeness and reproducibility of their research.
