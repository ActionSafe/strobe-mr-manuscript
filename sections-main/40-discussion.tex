% \subsection{Interpretation of findings}

% [Interpretation and context of the results]

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first large-scale automated evaluation of reporting quality in MR studies using an LLM aligned with the STROBE-MR guideline. By analyzing over 12,000 MR articles published between 2007 and 2025, several important patterns emerge regarding both the strengths and weaknesses in current reporting practices. Overall, MR studies demonstrate high compliance with some core STROBE-MR items, including research background, key results, findings and meanings of study. However, this contrast with the poor reporting of several methodological and transparency-related items, particularly in Other Information, Methods and Discussion sections. And the quality of reporting did not improve over time even after the STROBE-MR was introduced.

Our results are consistent with previous studies that assessed MR reporting on a smaller scale. A recent systematic review\cite{zuo_systematic_2026} of MR studies on risk factors for vascular dementia found that only 10 of the 26 articles (38\%) included a STROBE-MR checklist. Our findings also show that only 26.2\% articles published after 2021 cited STROBE-MR. Thus we could tell the use of STROBE-MR is not widely accepted in MR community and we must be cautious on the interpretation of MR results. For variants in different STROBE-MR sections and items, a previous study\cite{gibson_reporting_2023} that included 111 articles also identified a low reporting rate for items in the Methods section such as IV assumptions, details of MR estimators and covariates. One explanation for this poor reporting is the dominance of two-sample MR designs (88.6\%)-particularly in recent years. These articles rely on secondary data sources, which may lead authors to omit detailed descriptions of participants, data collection, and assumptions, assuming these are already understood or previously reported. However, such omissions hinder transparency and reproducibility, especially for readers who are not domain experts. Similar low reporting rates can also be found in the Other information section: 8\% of MR articles provided statistical code as reported in this study (12\% according to our study). Sharing of statistical code the is key to reproduce the results and thus a low sharing rate could affect the reproducibility of MR studies. The same study also found the reporting quality remained stable over time which is consistent with our findings.

Sensitivity analysis shows a substantial improvement of reporting rates in sections that are considered as poorly reported in main analysis. This can be explained by the fact that many studies do include relevant information but not all that are required by STROBE-MR items. Interestingly, we found that there was no difference in the average number of items reported between studies with and without self-claimed STROBE-MR compliance. These findings indicate that authors do have different interpretations of STROBE-MR, and the guideline was not used in a correct and consistent way.

These findings suggest that the introduction of the STROBE-MR guideline has not yet translated into uniformly improved reporting practices across the MR literature. Although certain items are well reported, key methodological and transparency elements remain neglected. This may reflect limited awareness of the guideline, lack of enforcement by journals and reviewers, or misunderstanding of certain items. To address these issues, stronger endorsement of STROBE-MR by journals/peer reviewers/funding bodies and more trainings on how to apply STROBE-MR may be necessary.

\subsection{Limitations}

% [Study limitations and potential biases]

The strengths of this study include its scale and the comprehensiveness of the MR literature assessments. However, there are also several limitations. First, the accuracy of LLM-based assessment depends on prompt design and also model selection: we only used GPT-5-mini, other models may not achieve the same results. Second, the reliance on available full texts on PubMed Central may introduce selection bias. Third, the study does not directly assess the correctness of reported methods, only their presence or absence. 

\subsection{Implications}
% [Clinical, policy, or research implications]
Our findings have several implications for various parties. For MR community, our results sugguest that important methodological details are not consistently reported across different studies and furthur efforts are needed to promote the use of STROBE-MR. For evidence synthesis researchers, our research provides a useful tool that can facilitate the rapid screening and identifying of well-reported MR studies. Our tool can also be integrated into journal submission workflows to help editors identifying any reporting problems and to provide authors with feedback. For reporting guideline researchers, our work demonstrates the potential of applying LLMs into large scale reporting quality monitoring which can then be used to improve the guideline itself. Finally, this study provides an automatic reporting assessment workflow that can be adapted to other reporting guidelines.
