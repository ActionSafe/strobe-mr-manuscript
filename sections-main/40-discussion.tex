% \subsection{Interpretation of findings}

% [Interpretation and context of the results]

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first large-scale automated evaluation of reporting quality in MR studies using an LLM aligned with the STROBE-MR guideline. By analyzing more than 12,000 MR articles published between 2007 and 2025, several important patterns emerged regarding both the strengths and weaknesses of current reporting practices. Overall, MR studies demonstrate high compliance with some core STROBE-MR items, including research background, key results, and the main findings and interpretations. However, this contrasts with the poor reporting of several methodological and transparency-related items, particularly in the Other Information, Methods, and Discussion sections. Reporting quality also did not improve over time, even after STROBE-MR was introduced.

Our results are consistent with previous studies that assessed MR reporting on a smaller scale. A recent systematic review \cite{zuo_systematic_2026} of MR studies on risk factors for vascular dementia found that only 10 of the 26 articles (38\%) included a STROBE-MR checklist. Our findings also show that only 26.2\% of articles published after 2021 cited STROBE-MR. This suggests that STROBE-MR is not widely accepted in the MR community, and the interpretation of MR results should therefore be cautious. Across different STROBE-MR sections and items, a previous study \cite{gibson_reporting_2023} that included 111 articles also identified low reporting rates for items in the Methods section, such as IV assumptions and details of MR estimators and covariates. One explanation for this poor reporting is the dominance of two-sample MR designs (88.6\%), particularly in recent years. These articles rely on secondary data sources, which may lead authors to omit detailed descriptions of participants, data collection, and assumptions, assuming that these are already understood or previously reported. However, such omissions hinder transparency and reproducibility, especially for readers who are not domain experts. Similar low reporting rates can also be found in the Other Information section: 8\% of MR articles provided statistical code in the study by Gibson et al. (12\% in our study). Sharing statistical code is key to reproducing results, and a low sharing rate could affect the reproducibility of MR studies. The same study also found that reporting quality remained stable over time, which is consistent with our findings.

Sensitivity analyses showed a substantial improvement in reporting rates in sections that were poorly reported in the main analysis. This may be because many studies include relevant information, but not all of it is captured by STROBE-MR items. Interestingly, we found no difference in the average number of items reported between studies with and without self-claimed STROBE-MR compliance. These findings indicate that authors have different interpretations of STROBE-MR, and that the guideline was not applied in a consistent way.

These findings suggest that the introduction of the STROBE-MR guideline has not yet translated into uniformly improved reporting practices across the MR literature. Although certain items are well reported, key methodological and transparency elements remain neglected. This may reflect limited awareness of the guideline, lack of enforcement by journals and reviewers, or misunderstanding of certain items. To address these issues, stronger endorsement of STROBE-MR by journals, peer reviewers, and funding bodies, along with more training on how to apply STROBE-MR, may be necessary.

\subsection{Limitations}

% [Study limitations and potential biases]

The strengths of this study include its scale and the comprehensiveness of the MR literature assessments. However, there are also several limitations. First, the reliance on available full texts on PubMed Central may introduce selection bias. Second, the accuracy of LLM-based assessment depends on prompt design and model selection: we only used GPT-5-mini, and other models may not achieve the same results. We also did not leverage the mutltimodal capabilities of the model, but the multimodality support is still in development and almost all information we needed for the assessment was available in text format.

\subsection{Implications}
% [Clinical, policy, or research implications]
Our findings have several implications for various parties. For the MR community, our results suggest that important methodological details are not consistently reported across different studies, and further efforts are needed to promote the use of STROBE-MR. For evidence synthesis researchers, our study provides a useful tool that can facilitate the rapid screening of and identification of well-reported MR studies. Our tool can also be integrated into journal submission workflows to help editors identify reporting problems and provide authors with feedback. For reporting guideline researchers, our work demonstrates the potential of applying LLMs to large-scale reporting quality monitoring, which can then be used to improve the guideline itself. Finally, this study provides an automated reporting assessment workflow that can be adapted to other reporting guidelines.
